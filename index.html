Reading it#

  • Narrow beats wide. Drag scales with width, thrust does not, so halving the width is worth roughly 1.4× the speed at the same thruster count.
  • Thrusters stack in series. There is no top speed — each adds thrust without adding drag, so long-and-narrow is the fast shape.
  • Width limits how many thrusters fit abreast. One is 4 tiles wide and needs a fifth for its fuel and oxidizer lines, so N of them want a hull at least 5N−1 wide. The tinted cells are below that: reaching them means stacking thrusters along the ship, past the exhaust dead zone, which is a deliberate build rather than a default one.
  • A platform never holds the baseline figure. It settles on one of two speeds 20 km/s apart, ±10 either side, according to which body is pulling on it — see below.
  • Outside 10–80% fill, nothing changes. The thruster's performance curve is defined by just those two endpoints and holds them outside that range, so 3% fill behaves as 10% and 95% as 80%. Empty is the exception rather than a very low setting: a thruster with no fluid in it produces nothing.

Why speed comes in two plateaus#

Gravity. Every planet carries gravity_pull of 0.1667… tiles per tick, and 60 ticks to the second makes that exactly 10 km/s. It acts toward whichever end of the leg you are nearer, so the origin drags for the first half and the destination pulls for the second:

LegBefore midpointAfter midpointNet
Planet to planet−10 km/s+10 km/sswitches at the midpoint
Planet out to the edge−10 km/s−10 km/s−10 the whole way
Edge back to a planet+10 km/s+10 km/s+10 the whole way

The solar system edge and the shattered planet carry a negative gravity_pull, so they push rather than attract — which is the whole reason their legs have no changeover. Outbound, the planet behind drags and the edge ahead pushes back, and both slow you. Returning, the edge pushes you away and the planet ahead pulls you in, and both help. Nothing in the rule above is special-cased for them; the sign does it.

Thruster fill has nothing to do with it; it is identical on both plateaus. The clearest demonstration is a platform with no fuel at all, which has no thrust to confuse the picture: it reads exactly −10.00 km/s and drifts back the way it came. A platform that runs out of propellant does not stop — it falls toward the nearest planet.

Hull size barely matters#

Hull size is measured in foundation tiles; weight follows from it, and machines and cargo are massless:

m=200⁢tiles+40000

Weight reaches cruise speed only through the 1 + m/10⁷ term, so it barely matters — it is acceleration that is inversely proportional to it. Eight times the hull costs 5.5% of cruise:

Where the numbers come from#

Cruise is where thrust balances drag — the acceleration expression set to zero, for N thrusters of thrust T, hull width w and weight m:

N⁢T1+m107=(1500⁢v2+1500⁢v)⋅w2+10000

Thrust per thruster is not exposed by the API. It was measured across platform classes at one throttle setpoint, and the page re-predicts each measured platform from the resulting constant — the build fails if any drifts over 1%.

Caveats#

  • Thrust per thruster was measured at 43% fill. The slider rescales it using the thruster performance curve.
  • Validated against a 10-wide hull with 2 legendary thrusters at weight 168,000: predicted baseline 391 against measured plateaus of 381.3 and 401.
  • platform.speed is tiles/tick; km/s = speed × 60, where 60 is the design UPS and 1 tile = 1 km.

The damage model#

applied=(base⁢(1+bonus)n−decrease)⋅(1−percent)

where n is 2 for bullets, because the same technology raises the ammo and the turret, and 1 for everything else. The flat decrease is subtracted before the percentage, and it is the whole story on large asteroids: big subtracts 2,000 from every physical hit and huge subtracts 3,000. No amount of bullet research clears that at a level anyone will reach, so any route spawning big asteroids needs rocket turrets.
